You already know the picture: Ada, Harbor Lights, free / held / blocked / ticket. These five answers are that picture under pressure. The short card is Five defenses.

1. Charged, but no ticket

He does not want this story. The card charge works. The app dies before it saves the ticket. Ten minutes later the hold ends. Someone else takes the seat. The first customer paid and has nothing.

We stop that in four steps.

  1. We write a payment row and save it before we call the card mock.
  2. While that payment is open, the seat stays with that customer. The 10-minute clock cannot give it away.
  3. If the charge succeeded, a later check writes the ticket. We do not charge again.
  4. If the check says the charge failed, the seat becomes free. They were not left holding a charge with no ticket.

Open means pending, unknown, or succeeded but the ticket is not written yet. The mock runs at most once for that payment.

Say this

I save the payment before I charge. If the charge works and the ticket is missing, the seat stays theirs until a later check writes the ticket. I do not charge a second time.

2. The hold ends while payment is running

There are two different moments. Do not mix them up.

  1. They never pressed checkout. The hold ends at 10 minutes. A waitlist hold ends at 15. The seat becomes free. Checkout cannot start after that clock.
  2. They pressed checkout and the payment row is saved. The 10 minutes do nothing. The seat stays held until we know the payment succeeded or failed.

If the app dies before that payment row is saved, nobody was charged. The same 10-minute clock still frees the seat.

Say this

The timer only frees a hold that has no payment. Once checkout has saved a payment, the seat stays with them until we know the result.

3. Six and twelve, at the same time

One order can have at most 6 seats. One customer at one event can have at most 12, counting holds and tickets together.

If the request asks for more seats than they have room for, we save zero holds. We do not save some of them.

Two requests from the same customer at the same event: we lock one row for that customer and event first. The second request waits. Then it counts again. If the first request used the last spots, the second saves nothing.

Two different customers, same seat: the database allows only one active hold or ticket for that seat at that event. The second insert fails. That customer gets no new hold.

Ada already has 10 tickets. She tries to hold 3 more. She has 2 spots left under 12. The request asks for 3, so she gets no new holds at all.

Say this

Six on one order. Twelve for that customer at that event. Same customer: I lock first, then count. Same seat, two people: the database keeps one.

4. The two state machines

A seat is free, held, blocked, or a ticket. That is the booking machine. You already practiced this in lesson 1.

  • Checkout leaves the seat held. It is not a ticket yet.
  • Success, including a later lookup, makes it a ticket.
  • Decline makes it free.
  • Unknown stays held until that lookup.

A refund is pending, unknown, succeeded, or failed. We save that row before we call the mock, same as a payment.

  • While it is pending or unknown, the ticket stays and the money has not come back.
  • A customer cancel that succeeds takes the ticket off. The seat can be held again.
  • If an admin cancels the event, the money comes back, including the fee, and the seats are not put back on sale.
  • An exceptional refund returns only the cents the admin named. The tickets stay.
  • Failed changes nothing. A repair uses those same cents. It does not pick a new time window.

Say this

A seat is free, held, blocked, or a ticket. A refund is pending, unknown, succeeded, or failed. Unknown does not finish either one. A lookup does.

5. A partial refund, discounts, and daylight saving

When payment succeeds, each ticket stores four numbers: its start price, its share of the group discount, its share of the promo, and its share of the fee. A later cancel uses those stored numbers. We do not split the order again.

Discounts are split by each ticket’s price. The fee is split in equal shares. A leftover cent goes to the ticket with the lowest id. A fee of 100 cents on three tickets is 34, 33, and 33.

The window is real time before the show, not the clock on the wall.

  1. 168 hours or more: they get the full net back. Net is the price minus that ticket’s discounts. The fee stays with us.
  2. From there down to exactly 48 hours: they get half the net. An odd number rounds up. The fee stays.
  3. Under 48 hours: they get nothing.

Daylight saving can make one local day 23 or 25 hours long. We compare two instants. A long local day does not add an extra hour to the refund window.

Ada cancels one ticket 50 hours before the show. Fifty sits between 48 and 168, so she gets half that ticket’s net. She does not get her share of the fee.

Say this

I refund the numbers already stored on that ticket. The window is real hours before the show, so daylight saving does not change it. A customer cancel does not return the fee.

Say it, then check

He describes a moment. You pick the outcome.

Each choice is the same length. The right one is not always first.

The charge succeeded. The app died before the ticket was saved. Nobody has run a lookup yet.

Checkout already saved the payment row. Then the 10-minute hold clock runs out.

Ada has room for 2 more seats. Two of her requests arrive together. The first one holds those 2. What does the second request save?

A customer cancel refund is still unknown. No lookup has finished.

She cancels one ticket 50 hours before the show. What does she get back?

The clocks change for daylight saving. How do we measure the refund window?
